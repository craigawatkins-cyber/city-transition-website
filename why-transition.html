Why this matters — and why now

Holiday Island isn't facing a crisis today. But the legal ground under Suburban Improvement Districts across Arkansas is shifting fast, and waiting until it becomes a crisis here means losing the chance to shape our own outcome.

A funding structure built for a different job

Holiday Island has operated as a Suburban Improvement District since 1970, under Arkansas Code Annotated §14-92-201 et seq. — a statute rooted in Act 41 of 1941. That law was written to fund the creation of infrastructure like roads, sewer, and water works, mostly in rural Arkansas. It was never designed to fund decades of ongoing maintenance and operations.

That mismatch has become the central legal weakness of improvement districts statewide: assessments can recover the cost of building something, but the statute offers no clean mechanism for funding its upkeep forever. Attempts by suburban and municipal recreational improvement districts to work around that gap have repeatedly ended up in lawsuits.

It isn't hypothetical — it's already happening nearby

Recent outcomes for Arkansas improvement districts
CommunityWhat happened
Horseshoe BendAs the result of a lawsuit, the SID has already been dissolved, with the city taking over recreational amenities in 2025.
Ozark AcresSettled a lawsuit by moving to consider incorporating and phasing out the SID by 2028.
Cherokee VillageA current judge's order requires the SID to be dissolved by the end of 2026.
Holiday IslandSettled one lawsuit in 2011 and is currently involved in another that must still be litigated.

It's worth saying plainly: none of these districts, including ours, ever intended to illegally exact a tax from property owners. They were simply trying to find a workable way to pay the bills within a statute that doesn't fully support that goal. Nevertheless, the lawsuits happened anyway.

The question worth asking

If several of our sister communities have already ended up with a court or a legal settlement deciding their future, isn't it worth Holiday Island thinking now about a similar possibility — while we still get to write the plan ourselves?

This is a choice point, not a doomsday

To be clear about what this site is and isn't: the Mayor's May 2026 presentation was explicitly framed as a feasibility study, not a finalized plan. Nothing here is decided. A sales-tax election and two separate resident petitions all stand between today and any actual transition — see the proposed timeline for exactly what would need to happen and when.

But the study's own framing is worth taking seriously: "It's better to have our plan than to have someone else's plan forced on us." Holiday Island can either shape this transition deliberately, on a timeline of its own choosing — or risk having a judge or a lawsuit settlement dictate the terms later, the way it's already played out in three neighboring communities.
