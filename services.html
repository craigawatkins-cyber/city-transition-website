Service by service: what it takes to run Holiday Island

Four services sit at the heart of this transition. Here's the honest state of each today, and what a transition to city ownership would mean.

Roads

71 miof city roads — vs. ~10 mi typical for a similar-size city
40%paved (asphalt/concrete), resurfaced every 25–30 years
60%chip-and-seal, needing resurfacing every 5–15 years
$700Kin paving grants received or awarded (not guaranteed)

Laid end to end, Holiday Island's 71 miles of roads would reach Springfield, Missouri heading north, or the Oklahoma border at Siloam Springs heading west. Almost all of it has roadside ditches that need regular maintenance, and that same mileage drives up costs for everything from grading to snow removal. The city has received one $300,000 paving grant and been awarded a second $400,000 grant expected in 2026 or 2027 — though grant money is never guaranteed.

Under the proposed timeline, the Road Department would be the very first service to transition to the city, as early as 2026, funded by a dedicated Street Fund (fuel-tax turnback, county road-tax return, and general fund support).

Water & Sewer

Just like the roads, Holiday Island has more than 71 miles of water and sewer lines to maintain. The current treatment plant bond matures in October 2031, but could realistically be paid off within about 18 months from now.

A new state requirement changes the math

New Arkansas legislation now requires municipal water and sewer systems to build and maintain a capital replacement reserve — funding for the inevitable cost of replacing lines, pumps, tanks, and treatment plants. Current rates don't generate enough revenue to meet that requirement, meaning a rate adjustment is coming either way, HISID or city. Under a transition, that increase would be partly offset once the sewer bond payment itself drops off the bill.

The proposed timeline has the Water Department transitioning to the city in 2027, once the sewer bond is retired.

Fire & Emergency Medical Response

$574,081Holiday Island's fire budget vs. ~$250,000 typical for a city our size
78%of February 2026 EMS calls were medical or lift-assist
67total fire calls in February 2026

Yes, Holiday Island's fire budget runs well above the typical small-city benchmark — and the study treats that as largely justified, not wasteful: our spread-out geography, an older population needing more medical response, and real difficulty recruiting volunteers (most residents are retired) all drive real costs. The bigger concern flagged by the study isn't the operating budget — it's that the current capital reserve to replace fire trucks and maintain all four fire stations is inadequate.

Fire department transition is proposed for 2027, alongside the public safety decision — see the Public Safety page for how police/sheriff and fire would be funded together.

Parks & Recreation

Today's recreational amenities — two golf courses, a marina, pools, trails, campgrounds, and a community center — require roughly $500,000 a year in subsidy from HISID assessment revenue. A city government cannot legally fund that kind of subsidy from general tax dollars, so recreation has to become self-funding through a dedicated fee if it's going to continue at anything like its current level.

The proposed plan

  • The underused 9-hole course would become a lower-maintenance mowed park area, with grant funding sought to repurpose it — freeing up resources to actually improve the primary 18-hole course.
  • The pool would become a public pool with lifeguards — free for paying Urban Service District members, pay-to-use for the general public.
  • The Clubhouse would become a community center — free for USD members' community functions.
  • Golf would keep its greens fees for all players, USD member or not.

For context: the national average for parks & recreation spending in a city of 2,500 is about $81.19 per resident, or roughly $203,000 a year — for the kind of parks and rec you'd find in a typical small town. Holiday Island's amenity level is intentionally well above that, because recreation and lifestyle are core to why people choose to live here — and to why they'll choose to move here in the future. See how that draw factors into the Growth Plan.
