A note on tone

Most of this site makes the case for transitioning services from HISID to the City. This page is different on purpose. Whether Holiday Island keeps contracting with the Carroll County Sheriff or forms its own police department is a genuinely open community decision — one the study itself leaves unresolved and hands to residents through a petition process. So here, we lay out both sides as evenly as we can.

Public Safety: Sheriff Contract or City Police Department?

Regardless of which option Holiday Island chooses, a Department of Public Safety would be created, combining law enforcement, the fire department, and code enforcement under one roof — funded by a new Urban Service District fee on the water bill.

Where things stand today

2deputies contracted, 80 hrs/week, at $31.28/hr (~$130,125/yr)
25%of February 2026 actually covered — 183 of 744 hours
9 daysin February 2026 had zero deputy coverage

The county has had difficulty consistently supplying the full 80 contracted hours a week, typically delivering more like 55–70 hours instead. Sheriff's deputies can enforce state law (traffic, criminal) in Holiday Island, but by law cannot enforce city ordinances — which can complicate code enforcement. The current contract expires at the end of 2026, and a renewal is expected to cost more.

Arguments residents have raised for a police department

The Mayor's July 2026 presentation laid out nine reasons Holiday Island might prefer a police department over the deputy contract. We're presenting them here as arguments to weigh, not as a conclusion:

1. Jurisdiction & service needs

A police department's authority stays within city boundaries — patrol focused entirely on Holiday Island, not shared countywide.

2. Cost & resource allocation

Direct local control over budget, staffing, and equipment, without competing for shared county resources.

3. Cost vs. coverage

Even at full contracted hours, over half the week has no deputy in town; the nearest one could be 40 miles away during a 911 call.

4. Community control

A police chief answers to the City Council, giving residents more direct accountability over hiring and policy.

5. Specialized local needs

71 miles of road with isolated homes make some residents more vulnerable and harder to reach quickly.

6. Traffic & crime deterrence

More consistent local patrol presence is argued to be a stronger deterrent than occasional coverage.

7. Revenue

Fine revenue from local traffic and criminal enforcement would return to the city instead of the county.

8. Code enforcement support

Sheriff's deputies legally cannot enforce city ordinances; city police could assist in situations that are potentially unsafe for code officers.

9. Community connection

Proponents point to Holiday Island's strong relationship with its fire department as a model a stable local police force could also build.

The legal options, and why "do nothing" isn't one of them

Under Arkansas law (A.C.A. §14-52-101 through 103), a city of the second class like Holiday Island may establish its own police department, may instead contract with an existing agency (the Sheriff, or in theory Eureka Springs PD via an interlocal agreement), or may establish a marshal's office by ordinance. What a second-class city cannot legally do is have no formal law enforcement arrangement at all.

  • Marshal's office: Legally requires the same police-academy (CLEST) certification and cost as a regular officer, so it offers little real savings. It's also rarely used statewide — only one Arkansas city of the second class has a marshal, and that city also maintains a full police department.
  • Interlocal agreement with Eureka Springs: Legally possible, but neither Eureka Springs nor the county is obligated to agree, and Holiday Island residents would have no vote in choosing the Eureka Springs mayor who'd oversee the arrangement.

The funding plan on the table would keep the existing sheriff contract through the first year of the Urban Service District, using the roughly $357,000 cost difference between a full police-department year and a deputy-contract year to fund the department's startup costs.

How this actually gets decided

This isn't a City Council vote — it's a resident petition process. A petition to form a Public Safety Urban Service District needs signatures from at least 25% of the electors who voted in the last gubernatorial election — 325 signatures. Signing that petition is effectively a vote for the police department option. If it fails to reach 325 signatures, a second petition would instead seek a sheriff/deputy interlocal Urban Service District. If neither petition succeeds, the fire department's transition to the city would also be put on hold.
