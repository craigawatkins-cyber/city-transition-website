What will this cost me?

Every property is different. Answer four quick questions for a planning estimate of how a completed transition could change your yearly costs. Want to check the math? Every figure is traced in Sources and Methodology at the bottom of this page.

Before you start: this is a planning estimate, not a bill. It uses earlier feasibility-study estimates from the May and July 2026 presentations1,2, not adopted city rates. Actual costs depend on a sales-tax election, petitions, annual budgets and City Council decisions still to come.
How this calculator relates to the Long Range Plan

It shows the end result, not the path. The calculator compares today with a completed transition. The changes would arrive in phases from 2027 on, and the "When each change arrives" table in your results shows how. See the assessment diagram.

City property tax: 4 mills today, 5 assumed. The City levies 4 mills now.3,4,5 The Long Range Plan's projections assume 5. That is an assumption, not an adopted rate, so the calculator counts only the one extra mill as new. The City Council sets the rate each year.

The two fees are preliminary planning estimates. They are not adopted charges. Which properties would pay them, and how they would be billed, will depend on the City's adopted ordinance; see how these fees are calculated and collected. The calculator applies neither fee to vacant lots. The Public Safety fee ($632 or $888 a year) is from the July 2026 study. The Long Range Plan gives $600 to $672 a year for the sheriff option4,2 and no figure for a police department. The Recreation fee ($187.85) is from the May 2026 study; the plan says that fee will be set in 2029.4

The sales tax is a ballot measure. Voters decide on the proposed 2.5% tax on November 3, 2026. The calculator includes it as if it passes.

The full detail is in Sources and Methodology.

Which number do you have for your real estate?

Not sure? Use market value. Arkansas generally assesses property at 20% of market value. However, Amendment 79 may limit increases in assessed value for qualifying properties. If you know your actual taxable assessed value, enter it for a more individualized estimate.

Look for "Taxable Value" on your Carroll County property-tax record and enter it as shown. The calculator does not apply the 20% again.

Enter the "Estimated Market Value" from your county assessor record: land plus any home or structure. The calculator works out the 20% assessed value for you.

Which number should I enter?

Your county record may show three different values for the same property. Here is a made-up example:

Which value goes with which option
Assessor's fieldExample amount
Estimated Market Value ← market value option$300,000
Full Assessed Value (20% of market value) — don't enter this$60,000
Taxable Value ← taxable value option$52,000

In this example, one additional mill is $60 a year using market value, or $52 a year using taxable value. The taxable value is the one a tax bill is based on, so it gives the closer estimate.

How is the city property tax calculated?

A mill is $1 of tax for every $1,000 of assessed value, and Arkansas assesses real estate at 20% of market value. So one mill costs $60 a year on a $300,000 home.

The City levies 4 mills today ($240 a year on that home). The Long Range Plan assumes 5 ($300). The calculator counts only the $60 difference as new. If the City Council keeps the rate at 4 mills, this line would be $0.

The legal background is in Sources and Methodology.

Enter the assessed value of your personal property from your Carroll County assessment record, not its estimated market value. Use the total for your vehicles and other taxable personal property. If your assessed personal property value is $9,440, one additional mill would equal $9.44 annually.

Where do I find this number?

Arkansas assesses personal property at 20% of market value. Your county assessment statement has already calculated this assessed value, so you do not need to estimate market value or calculate the 20% assessment yourself.

Add together the assessed values of the vehicles and other items listed on your statement, and enter the total. Illustrative example — not a specific household:

4. Your annual taxable spending (optional)

Leave this blank to use the study's estimate of $270 a year, which assumes $10,800 of taxable spending. Or enter your own yearly figure and the calculator applies the proposed 2.5%.

What counts as taxable spending?

It's what you spend in a year on purchases the City's proposed 2.5% sales tax would apply to. Only this new tax is counted, not the state and county sales tax you already pay.

In general, a store purchase is taxed where the store is, a delivery where it is delivered, and a taxable service where it is performed (Arkansas DFA sales and use tax FAQs). Not every purchase or service is taxed, so your cost depends on what you buy and where.

The default $270 is a modeling assumption from the feasibility study, not a measured average for Holiday Island households. It works out to $10,800 a year, about $900 a month.

Putting the number in context

What it pays for

Sheriff or police coverage, the fire department, about 69 miles of roads, and the golf courses, marina, pools and trails. The costs are not new; the way they are paid for is.

Standing still is not free

Today's bill reflects today's structure, not the permanent cost of doing nothing. The Long Range Plan projects that HISID's current assessment capacity could be exhausted around 2037 under its assumptions, and roads, public safety and recreation need funding under any structure.

    A note for residents on a fixed income

    If you're retired, on Social Security, or managing a fixed monthly budget, "new tax" understandably sounds like unwelcome news no matter how the rest of this site frames it. That reaction deserves a straight answer, not a sales pitch.

    A note on this section: the material below isn't from the Mayor's feasibility study. It's independently researched from the Arkansas Department of Finance and Administration (property tax relief programs) and the National Recreation and Park Association (public support for parks and recreation). Confirm your own eligibility with the Carroll County Assessor's office.

    What "ad valorem" means — and why it changes who pays what

    Ad valorem is Latin for "according to value." It's the standard term for a tax calculated as a percentage of what something is worth, rather than a flat dollar amount charged to everyone alike. City property tax, modeled here at the 5-mill planning assumption, is an ad valorem tax: every property is taxed at the same rate — 0.1% of its estimated market value — so the dollar amount scales with what the property is actually worth. HISID's current assessment works the opposite way: it's a flat, per-property charge that doesn't look at value at all, so a modest home and a much larger one on the lake pay the identical dollar amount today.

    Same flat assessment today, regardless of value
    Charge$150,000 home$850,000 lakefront home
    Today's flat HISID assessment$834.60$834.60
    ...as a share of the home's value0.56%0.10%
    City ad valorem property tax at 5 mills (planning assumption)$150.00$850.00
    ...as a share of the home's value0.10%0.10%

    Today, both homes pay the exact same $834.60 HISID assessment — which means, measured against what each home is worth, the $150,000 home is paying more than five times the effective rate of the $850,000 home. Under an ad valorem property tax, both properties pay the same 0.1% rate: the lakefront home pays more in dollars, as you'd expect from a property worth over five times as much, but the two homes carry the same proportional burden. This fairness argument is about how the property-tax component is distributed, not about whether your total bill goes up or down overall — use the calculator above for your own full picture.

    The flat assessment you pay today has no relief built in. Real property tax does.

    HISID's current assessment is a flat charge — everyone in a given category pays the same amount, with no discount for income or age. Arkansas's homestead tax credit and constitutional property-tax protections apply to the standard annual property tax billed on assessed value, not to a special-district assessment like HISID's. They already apply to eligible Holiday Island homeowners under the existing property-tax system, and they continue to apply as the City's tax structure evolves. The proposed additional city millage does not create a new homestead credit or a new eligibility category. Here is what those protections are:

    The Homestead Tax Credit

    Arkansas gives eligible homeowners a credit on the property tax for their primary residence.

    Arkansas increased the homestead property-tax credit from $600 to $675 beginning with the 2026 assessment year. As a result, property-tax receipts from earlier years may show the previous $600 credit.

    The credit lowers what you owe; it doesn't change the millage rate. You don't get an extra credit because a city millage is added.

    This calculator does not subtract the homestead credit. Check your current amount with the Carroll County Assessor.

    Source: Arkansas DFA — Property Tax Relief Programs; Act 174 of 2026 (HB1103).

    The 65+ / disabled assessment freeze

    Under Arkansas Amendment 79, homeowners who qualify for the homestead tax credit and who are age 65 or older, or disabled, may be eligible to have the taxable assessed value of their homestead frozen.

    The freeze is not automatic. Eligible homeowners must apply with the county assessor's office.

    A frozen assessed value may still increase if there is new construction or a substantial improvement to the property. A buyer does not inherit the previous owner's limitation.

    The freeze applies to assessed value, not to the tax bill. Millage rates, Urban Service District fees, and other charges can still change, so it does not guarantee that your total bill stays flat.

    Source: Arkansas Assessment Coordination Division — Property Tax Relief. Confirm eligibility and how to apply with the Carroll County Assessor.

    The 5% annual assessment growth limitation

    Arkansas Amendment 79 limits how fast a property's assessed value can rise because of a countywide reappraisal.

    For a qualifying homestead used as a primary residence, the limit is generally 5% a year. For other property, including vacant lots, it is generally 10% a year. Both apply until the property reaches its full assessed value.

    New construction, substantial improvements and newly discovered property are treated differently.

    This limits growth in assessed value. It does not cap your total tax bill, which also depends on millage rates and fees.

    Source: Arkansas Assessment Coordination Division — Property Tax Relief (Amendment 79).

    The one part of this bill that flexes with you

    Unlike a flat assessment or a fixed fee, the proposed 2.5% sales tax would only apply to what you actually spend — a lighter spending year means a smaller amount of this specific cost, the same way it would with any sales tax. A flat fee does not vary in that way. Revenue from a local sales tax may support municipal services and infrastructure; how much it raises depends on taxable spending in the city by residents and visitors.

    Why the recreation fee would apply whether or not you use the amenities

    Not everyone golfs, boats, or swims — and it's a fair question why a property owner would pay the proposed Recreation Urban Service District fee regardless. The reasoning usually given is that community amenities can affect a community's appeal as a whole, not only the people who use them. The points below summarize that reasoning and its limits.

    They can support your home's value

    Research suggests that parks, recreational facilities, golf courses, marinas, and other community amenities can contribute to neighborhood desirability and quality of life. The actual effect on property values varies by location, property characteristics, market conditions, and the quality and accessibility of the amenities.

    For Holiday Island, the golf courses, marina, pools, trails, and other recreational facilities are part of what distinguishes the community from other residential areas.

    These amenities do not guarantee a particular increase in property value, but maintaining them may help preserve the community's appeal to current and future homeowners.

    They're part of what attracts people to a community

    According to the National Recreation and Park Association, 90% of U.S. adults consider parks and recreation an important local government service. That is a public-opinion finding about local government services, not a statistic about home buying or property values.

    Holiday Island's lake access, golf courses, marina, pools and trails help distinguish it from surrounding communities. Maintaining them may contribute to the community's long-term attractiveness to new residents and visitors.

    Letting them decline could have broader economic consequences

    Holiday Island's recreation facilities are part of the community's identity. Allowing recreational amenities to deteriorate could have broader economic consequences, including reduced community desirability, deferred maintenance costs, and potential effects on surrounding property values.

    The actual impact would depend on what happens to the facilities and surrounding properties. Maintaining them may help preserve the community's appeal, but it does not guarantee that property values will rise.

    Source: National Recreation and Park Association, "Parks and Recreation Is Essential" (public opinion on parks and recreation as a local government service).

    None of that erases the honest fact that the Recreation and Public Safety fees, as currently estimated, are flat charges with no income-based discount — that's still true, and worth saying plainly rather than glossing over. But "I don't use it" and "it doesn't benefit me" aren't quite the same thing here.

    None of this changes the honest bottom line either: for most improved properties, this transition is a net cost increase, not a decrease. Use the calculator above with your own numbers to see your real one, not a hypothetical. But it's not an increase without guardrails — and if it turns out to be more than the community is willing to accept, the built-in off-ramps — the November vote and two later petitions — exist specifically so residents can say so.

    How the six line items are calculated

    The calculator adds together six line items using the source study's flat fees, with two adjustments: the study counted a full 5-mill city property tax as new, and this calculator counts only the one mill above the 4 the City already levies; and the study charged vacant lots the Recreation fee, which this calculator does not. Your net annual change is simply the sum of these six numbers. The Public Safety fees are the July 2026 (Session 2) estimates; the published examples further down let you cross-check against the original May 2026 presentation.

    Important: By default this calculator reproduces the simplified property-tax methodology used in the Holiday Island feasibility study, which applies the 20% assessment ratio to market value. Actual Arkansas property taxes are calculated using taxable assessed value and applicable local millage rates. Amendment 79 and other Arkansas property-tax provisions can cause a property's taxable assessed value to differ from a simple 20% calculation, which is why you can enter your actual taxable assessed value instead. Your actual future tax bill may still differ from this estimate. This calculator is not an official Arkansas tax calculator, and the figures below are not final city rates.

    Adopted rates, plan assumptions and legal notes

    • What is adopted and what is not. The City's four-mill levy for 2026 collections is adopted. The five-mill figure is a Long Range Plan assumption; future millage depends on annual City Council decisions and applicable legal requirements. The Recreation and Public Safety fees are preliminary proposed amounts. Nothing shown as a new charge has been adopted.
    • The five-mill limit. Five mills is the limit the Arkansas Constitution (Article 12, § 4) places on a city's own general property-tax levy.6 It applies to the municipal levy only. County, school district and other taxing jurisdictions levy their own millage, so the total millage on a property-tax bill is much higher than five mills.
    • Amendment 79. With the market-value method, the estimate uses the standard 20% assessment ratio and does not model Amendment 79 assessment limitations for your property. With the taxable-value method, it uses the taxable assessed value you enter and applies no further ratio.
    • Credits. The result is an estimate before the homestead credit and any other individual credits or tax adjustments.
    • Personal property. The estimate uses one additional mill as a planning assumption. Actual personal property millage may be subject to applicable Arkansas statutory adjustments (see Ark. Code Ann. § 26-26-405) and should not be read as a guaranteed future rate.
    • Road allocation. The separate 1.5-mill road allocation that goes to the City from the county road tax is part of the existing bill and is not expected to change, so it is not counted.
    • Sales tax. The feasibility study states the $270 household estimate; the $10,800 of taxable spending is this site's arithmetic from it ($270 ÷ 2.5%). Both are modeling assumptions. If voters approve the tax, when it takes effect depends on applicable legal requirements and the actual implementation schedule.
    • Earlier versions. The feasibility study, and earlier versions of this page, counted all five mills as new.

    Market value, assessed value, and taxable value — three different numbers

    Arkansas county assessors track three distinct figures for every property. Knowing which one is which matters for this calculator — and for reading your own assessment notice.

    Market value

    What the county estimates the property is worth. This is the number this calculator asks for by default.

    Full assessed value

    Generally 20% of market value for Arkansas real property. Do not enter this figure for your home — the calculator already applies the 20% ratio for you. Personal property is the exception: there you enter the assessed value from your county statement.

    Taxable value

    The assessed value actually used to calculate a real property tax bill, after Amendment 79's limitations on assessment increases are applied. This can legitimately be lower than the full assessed value. You can enter it in the calculator by choosing the taxable assessed value option.
